# John Deere’s Digital Pivot: Can a 187-Year-Old Manufacturer Become a Tech Platform?

## Introduction

In early 2023, John Deere CEO John May faced a defining question: could a company known for tractors and heavy machinery successfully reinvent itself as a technology platform company?

For nearly two centuries, Deere’s identity had been built on engineering excellence, manufacturing scale, and a deep understanding of farmers’ operational needs. But the industry was changing. Advances in artificial intelligence, data analytics, and connectivity were transforming agriculture into a highly data-driven sector. Competitors were investing in digital tools, startups were entering the market with software-first solutions, and farmers were under increasing pressure to improve efficiency and sustainability.

Deere had already begun investing heavily in precision agriculture, automation, and cloud-connected equipment. Yet the shift introduced new tensions. Farmers questioned who owned the data generated by their machines. Policymakers and advocacy groups challenged Deere’s restrictions on equipment repair. Meanwhile, the company was committing billions of dollars to technologies with uncertain adoption timelines.

As May stated in public interviews, “We’re really a technology company at this point.” The question was no longer whether Deere could innovate—it was whether it could successfully redefine itself without losing the trust of its core customers.

## The Protagonist: John May

John May became CEO of John Deere in 2019 after nearly two decades with the company. His career at Deere spanned finance, operations, and leadership roles, giving him a broad understanding of both the company’s legacy strengths and its future challenges.

Unlike previous leaders who focused primarily on manufacturing efficiency and product development, May recognized that the future of agriculture would be shaped by data. He believed that Deere’s competitive advantage would come not only from building machines, but from integrating those machines into a broader ecosystem of software, analytics, and services.

This vision required a fundamental shift in strategy. Deere would no longer simply sell equipment—it would become a provider of technology-enabled solutions. Under May’s leadership, the company began investing aggressively in digital capabilities, including artificial intelligence, cloud platforms, and autonomous systems.

## The Digital Transformation

John Deere’s transformation centered around precision agriculture—a model that uses data, sensors, GPS, and machine learning to optimize farming operations.

The company made several key investments to support this shift:

- Autonomous tractors capable of operating without human drivers, publicly unveiled at CES 2022  
- Acquisition of Blue River Technology in 2017 to expand machine learning capabilities, particularly in computer vision and crop analysis  
- Development of the John Deere Operations Center, a cloud-based platform used by farmers to collect, analyze, and manage field data  
- Expansion of precision agriculture offerings, now a core part of Deere’s Production & Precision Agriculture segment  

These investments were supported by significant financial commitment. By 2023, John Deere reported approximately $61 billion in net sales and continued to invest over $2 billion annually in research and development. A growing portion of this investment was directed toward digital technologies, including automation, artificial intelligence, and connected systems.

The transformation also introduced a new business model. Traditionally, Deere generated revenue through one-time equipment sales. With the rise of digital tools, the company began shifting toward recurring revenue streams, including software subscriptions, data analytics services, and ongoing platform support.

For farmers, the value proposition was clear: improved efficiency, reduced input costs, and higher yields. For Deere, the opportunity was to build a long-term relationship with customers through continuous service delivery.

## Strategic Tensions

Despite the potential benefits, Deere’s transformation introduced several significant challenges.

### Hardware vs. Software Identity

Deere’s legacy as a manufacturing company remained a core part of its identity. Transitioning to a software-driven business raised questions about whether it could compete with technology firms while maintaining its engineering strengths.

This tension was both internal and external. Internally, the company needed to develop new capabilities in software development, data analytics, and platform management. Externally, customers and investors needed to understand how Deere’s value proposition was evolving.

### Data Ownership and Trust

As Deere collected increasing amounts of data through connected equipment, questions emerged around ownership and control. Farmers were concerned that their operational data could be used in ways that did not benefit them.

Data became both an asset and a source of tension. While it enabled better decision-making and optimization, it also raised concerns about privacy, transparency, and competitive advantage.

### Right-to-Repair Movement

Deere faced growing backlash over its restrictions on equipment repair. The company limited access to certain software and diagnostic tools, arguing that these controls were necessary for safety and intellectual property protection.

Farmers and advocacy groups disagreed, pushing for the right to repair their own equipment. The issue gained national attention, leading to negotiations between Deere and the American Farm Bureau Federation. In 2023, the two parties reached a memorandum of understanding aimed at addressing some of these concerns.

### Investment Risk and Adoption Uncertainty

Deere’s digital transformation required substantial investment in emerging technologies. While these investments positioned the company for long-term growth, they also introduced financial risk.

Adoption rates varied among farmers, particularly smaller operations that may have been less willing or able to invest in new technologies. This created uncertainty around the timing and scale of returns on Deere’s investments.

## Industry Context

The agriculture industry is undergoing a structural shift driven by several key factors:

- Labor shortages, increasing the need for automation  
- Rising input costs, driving demand for efficiency  
- Climate variability, requiring more precise and adaptive farming practices  
- Growing global food demand  

Technology has become a central driver of change. Industry reports indicate that agriculture is becoming increasingly data-driven, with connected devices, artificial intelligence, and cloud platforms playing a critical role in improving productivity and sustainability.

By 2023, John Deere’s Production & Precision Agriculture segment had become one of the company’s largest business units, reflecting the growing importance of digital capabilities. Competitors such as CNH Industrial and AGCO were also investing in precision agriculture, while new entrants focused on software solutions were reshaping the competitive landscape.

## The Decision Point

As Deere moved deeper into its transformation, John May and his leadership team faced a critical decision: how aggressively should the company pursue its evolution into a technology platform?

A more aggressive approach could accelerate innovation and strengthen Deere’s competitive position, but it also risked alienating customers and increasing financial exposure. A more cautious approach could preserve relationships and manage risk, but it might leave the company vulnerable to faster-moving competitors.

The decision would shape not only Deere’s future, but also the broader evolution of the agriculture industry.
