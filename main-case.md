# John Deere’s Digital Pivot: Can a 187-Year-Old Manufacturer Become a Tech Platform?

## Introduction

In early 2023, John Deere CEO John May faced a defining question: could a company known for tractors and heavy machinery successfully reinvent itself as a technology platform company?

For nearly two centuries, Deere had built its reputation on engineering excellence, manufacturing scale, and a deep connection to the agricultural community. Its machines powered farms across the world, and its brand was synonymous with reliability and durability. However, the broader agricultural landscape was undergoing a fundamental shift. Advances in artificial intelligence, machine learning, and data analytics were transforming agriculture into a highly digital, data-driven sector.

Farmers were facing increasing pressure to produce more with fewer resources. Rising input costs, labor shortages, and climate variability made traditional farming methods less sustainable. At the same time, new technologies offered the potential to improve efficiency, reduce waste, and increase yields. This combination of pressure and opportunity created a turning point for the industry.

John Deere had already begun investing heavily in precision agriculture, automation, and cloud-connected equipment. Yet the transformation introduced a new set of challenges. Farmers began questioning who owned the data generated by connected machines. Advocacy groups pushed for the right to repair equipment without restrictions. Internally, Deere was evolving from a manufacturing-focused organization into one that required capabilities in software development, data science, and platform management.

As May stated in public interviews, “We’re really a technology company at this point.” The statement reflected both ambition and uncertainty. Deere’s future would no longer be defined solely by the machines it built, but by the data and insights those machines could generate.

The question was no longer whether Deere could innovate—it was whether it could successfully transform without losing the trust of its customers or overextending its investments.

## The Protagonist: John May

John May became CEO of John Deere in 2019 after nearly two decades with the company. His experience spanned finance, operations, and leadership roles across multiple business units, giving him a comprehensive understanding of both the company’s strengths and its limitations.

May inherited a company with a strong market position but facing increasing pressure to adapt. He recognized that the future of agriculture would be shaped not just by physical equipment, but by the integration of hardware, software, and data. His vision was to transform Deere into a “smart industrial” company that could deliver value through connected systems and data-driven insights.

This shift required a different approach to strategy. Rather than focusing solely on product development, Deere needed to build an ecosystem that combined machinery, software, and analytics. This meant investing in new technologies, developing new capabilities, and redefining how the company interacted with its customers.

May’s leadership style emphasized long-term thinking and strategic investment. He understood that digital transformation would not deliver immediate returns, but he believed it was essential for maintaining competitiveness in a rapidly evolving industry.

## The Digital Transformation

John Deere’s transformation centered around precision agriculture—a model that uses data, sensors, GPS, and artificial intelligence to optimize farming operations.

The company made several significant investments to support this transition:

- In 2017, Deere acquired Blue River Technology, a company specializing in machine learning and computer vision, to enhance its ability to analyze crops and optimize inputs  
- In 2022, Deere unveiled a fully autonomous tractor at the Consumer Electronics Show (CES), signaling its commitment to automation and advanced technology  
- The company expanded the John Deere Operations Center, a cloud-based platform that allows farmers to collect, store, and analyze data from their equipment  
- Precision agriculture became a central component of Deere’s Production & Precision Agriculture segment, reflecting its growing importance within the business  

These investments required substantial financial resources. By 2023, John Deere reported approximately $61 billion in net sales and invested over $2 billion annually in research and development. A significant portion of this investment was directed toward digital technologies, including artificial intelligence, automation, and cloud infrastructure.

The transformation also reshaped Deere’s business model. Historically, the company generated revenue through one-time equipment sales. With the introduction of digital tools and services, Deere began shifting toward recurring revenue streams, including software subscriptions and data analytics services.

For farmers, these technologies offered tangible benefits. Precision agriculture enabled more efficient use of inputs such as water, fertilizer, and labor, leading to improved yields and reduced costs. For Deere, the opportunity was to establish ongoing relationships with customers through continuous service delivery.

## Organizational and Cultural Challenges

Transforming into a technology-driven company required significant internal change. Deere’s traditional strengths in manufacturing did not automatically translate into expertise in software development or data analytics.

The company needed to build new capabilities while maintaining its existing operations. This required hiring new talent, retraining employees, and fostering collaboration between engineering, software, and data teams.

The shift also introduced cultural challenges. Manufacturing organizations typically operate with long product development cycles, while software development emphasizes rapid iteration and continuous improvement. Aligning these approaches required changes in processes, communication, and decision-making.

Leadership had to balance competing priorities. While the long-term strategy focused on digital transformation, short-term performance remained tied to equipment sales. Managing this balance required careful coordination and clear messaging across the organization.

## Customer Adoption and Behavior

The success of Deere’s transformation depended heavily on customer adoption. While large-scale farming operations were more likely to adopt precision agriculture technologies, smaller farms often faced financial and operational barriers.

Trust played a critical role in adoption. Farmers needed confidence that the data collected by Deere’s systems would be used responsibly and in their best interest. Concerns about data ownership and privacy created hesitation among some customers.

The shift to subscription-based services also required a change in purchasing behavior. Farmers who were accustomed to making one-time equipment purchases now needed to consider ongoing costs associated with software and analytics platforms.

These factors influenced the pace of adoption and created uncertainty for Deere as it invested in digital capabilities.

## Strategic Tensions

### Hardware vs Software Identity
Deere’s transformation raised fundamental questions about its identity. Could it successfully compete as a technology company while maintaining its strengths as a manufacturer?

### Data Ownership Concerns
As agriculture became more data-driven, control over data emerged as a critical issue. Farmers wanted transparency and control, while Deere sought to leverage data to improve its offerings.

### Right-to-Repair Movement
Restrictions on repair and software access led to backlash from farmers and advocacy groups, resulting in public debate and policy discussions.

### Investment Risk
Significant investment in emerging technologies created uncertainty around returns, particularly given varying adoption rates.

## Competitive and Industry Pressure

John Deere’s transformation did not occur in isolation. Traditional competitors such as CNH Industrial and AGCO expanded their own precision agriculture capabilities, seeking to keep pace with Deere’s innovation.

At the same time, new entrants—including technology startups—introduced software-driven solutions that focused on data analytics and farm management. These companies often operated with greater agility, allowing them to innovate quickly and target specific aspects of the agricultural value chain.

The combination of traditional and new competitors increased pressure on Deere to maintain its leadership position while continuing to invest in innovation.

## Financial and Strategic Implications

John Deere’s transformation had significant financial implications. While the company’s scale provided the resources to invest in digital technologies, it also increased expectations for performance.

The transition to a service-oriented model introduced new revenue opportunities but also required changes in cost structure and pricing strategies. Developing and maintaining digital platforms involved ongoing expenses, including software development, data storage, and customer support.

From a strategic perspective, success would allow Deere to establish a dominant position in precision agriculture, creating a competitive advantage based on data, integration, and long-term customer relationships. Failure, however, could result in overinvestment, reduced customer trust, and increased competition.

## The Decision Point

As Deere moved forward with its transformation, John May and his leadership team faced a critical decision: how aggressively should the company pursue its evolution into a technology platform?

An aggressive approach could accelerate innovation and strengthen Deere’s competitive position, but it also risked alienating customers and increasing financial exposure. A more cautious approach could preserve stability but limit growth and innovation.

The decision would shape the future of the company and its role in the evolving agriculture industry.
