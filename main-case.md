# John Deere’s Digital Pivot: Can a 187-Year-Old Manufacturer Become a Tech Platform?

## Introduction

In early 2023, John Deere CEO John May faced a defining question: could a company known for tractors and heavy machinery successfully reinvent itself as a technology platform company?

For nearly two centuries, Deere’s identity had been built on engineering excellence, manufacturing scale, and a deep understanding of farmers’ operational needs. But the industry was changing. Advances in artificial intelligence, data analytics, and connectivity were transforming agriculture into a highly data-driven sector. Competitors were investing in digital tools, startups were entering the market with software-first solutions, and farmers were under increasing pressure to improve efficiency and sustainability.

Deere had already begun investing heavily in precision agriculture, automation, and cloud-connected equipment. Yet the shift introduced new tensions. Farmers questioned who owned the data generated by their machines. Policymakers and advocacy groups challenged Deere’s restrictions on equipment repair. Meanwhile, the company was committing billions of dollars to technologies with uncertain adoption timelines.

As May described the shift, “We’re really a technology company at this point.” The company’s future no longer depended on steel and horsepower—it depended on data.

## The Protagonist: John May

John May became CEO of John Deere in 2019 after nearly two decades with the company. His career at Deere spanned finance, operations, and leadership roles, giving him a broad understanding of both the company’s legacy strengths and its future challenges.

Unlike previous leaders who focused primarily on manufacturing efficiency and product development, May recognized that the future of agriculture would be shaped by data. He believed that Deere’s competitive advantage would come not only from building machines, but from integrating those machines into a broader ecosystem of software, analytics, and services.

This vision required a fundamental shift in strategy. Deere would no longer simply sell equipment—it would become a provider of technology-enabled solutions. Under May’s leadership, the company began investing aggressively in digital capabilities, including artificial intelligence, cloud platforms, and autonomous systems.

## The Digital Transformation

John Deere’s transformation centered around precision agriculture—a model that uses data, sensors, GPS, and machine learning to optimize farming operations.

Key investments included:
- Autonomous tractors capable of operating without human drivers, publicly unveiled at CES 2022  
- Acquisition of Blue River Technology in 2017 to expand machine learning capabilities  
- Development of the John Deere Operations Center, a cloud-based platform used by farmers to manage field data  
- Expansion of precision agriculture offerings, now a core part of Deere’s Production & Precision Agriculture segment  

These investments were supported by significant financial commitment. By 2023, John Deere reported approximately $61 billion in net sales and continued to invest over $2 billion annually in research and development.

These innovations allowed farmers to increase efficiency, reduce costs, and improve crop yields. However, they also fundamentally changed Deere’s business model. Instead of relying solely on equipment sales, Deere began shifting toward recurring revenue through software and data services.

## Organizational and Cultural Challenges

Beyond strategy and technology, Deere’s transformation required internal change. Moving from a manufacturing-centric organization to a technology-driven company meant developing new capabilities in software engineering and data science.

This shift required changes in how teams collaborated and how products were developed. Manufacturing cycles differ significantly from software iteration cycles, creating operational complexity. Deere also needed to retrain employees and integrate new talent, leading to cultural challenges.

Leadership had to balance long-term transformation goals with short-term performance expectations tied to equipment sales.

## Customer Adoption and Behavior

Adoption of Deere’s digital tools varied among farmers. Larger operations were more likely to invest, while smaller farms faced financial barriers.

Trust was a major factor. Farmers needed confidence that their data would be used responsibly. The shift to subscription-based services also introduced new purchasing behavior, requiring farmers to adapt to ongoing costs.

## Strategic Tensions

### Hardware vs Software Identity
Deere’s transition raised questions about its identity and competitive positioning.

### Data Ownership Concerns
Farmers were increasingly concerned about data control and usage.

### Right-to-Repair Movement
Restrictions on repairs led to backlash and policy discussions.

### Investment Risk
Heavy investment in emerging technologies introduced financial uncertainty.

## Industry Context

Agriculture is undergoing a transformation driven by labor shortages, rising costs, and sustainability demands.

By 2023, Deere’s Production & Precision Agriculture segment had become one of its largest business units, reflecting a shift toward digital solutions.

## Competitive Pressure

Competitors like CNH Industrial and AGCO expanded digital capabilities, while startups introduced software-driven solutions.

## The Decision Point

Deere’s leadership faced a critical decision: how aggressively to pursue digital transformation.

The path forward involved balancing innovation, risk, customer trust, and long-term strategy.
